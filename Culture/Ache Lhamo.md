# Ache Lhamo

Ache Lhamo (a lce lha mo, often a che lha mo) is Tibet's narrative theatre. English calls it Tibetan opera. In 2009 UNESCO inscribed it on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The inscription recognizes a living form and a safeguarding effort. It does not freeze one troupe's version as the only one.

## What a performance contains

A performance mixes story, aria, speech, dance, comedy, and bright costume, traditionally outdoors. The classic central Tibetan accompaniment is two players: a drummer with a double-headed frame drum on a handle (often lag rnga) and a cymbal player (rol mo or sbub chal). The drummer is the master of ceremonies. Pattern, speed, and variation tell characters when to enter, leave, and move. The cymbals follow the drum. That division of labor is the finding of fieldwork with the Nepal Tibetan Lhamo Association, and it matches the wider description of the Lhasa-style tradition.

Arias are called rnam thar (namthar). Many Tibetans know famous ones well enough to sing them at a gathering, apart from any full play. Comic attendants puncture the high style. A full traditional show is long. Festival performances can run through a day.

Later professional companies, in Tibet and in exile, have sometimes added piwang, yangqin, dranyen, flute, or gyaling. Those colors are a modern expansion. Drum and cymbals are the part you cannot remove and still have the old form.

## How the tradition tells its beginning

Oral accounts credit the fourteenth-century yogi and bridge-builder Thangtong Gyalpo (Thang stong rgyal po), born in 1361 near Shigatse. The story says he used song and dance by seven sisters to gather support for iron bridges. Audiences called the performers lha mo, goddesses, and a che lha mo, goddess sisters, is the name that stayed. Treat this as the tradition's origin account. It is the account TIPA teaches. It is not a fourteenth-century prompt book.

## Lineages TIPA names

TIPA trains performers in three transmitted lines, each tied to plays:

| Lineage | Plays TIPA names |
| --- | --- |
| Kyormulung | Sukyi Nyima, Pema Woebar, Drowa Sangmo |
| Chungpa | Prince Norsang |
| Monru Ponsang | Dhepa Tenpa |

Other English sources romanize these names differently, and some lists of "three schools" do not match this one word for word. Use TIPA's list when you mean TIPA's teaching. Use a troupe's own name when you mean that troupe. Scripts are khrab gzhung. Transmission includes the script and the melody, and also the timing, the mask, the entrance, and how to shorten a story for an occasion.

The old Shoton roster at the Norbulingka included several major and minor companies. Exile communities rebuilt a Shoton in Dharamsala. In 1981 Jamyang Norbu wrote a new lhamo, *Chaksam* (*lcags zam*, "The Iron Bridge"), on Thangtong Gyalpo, with melodies adapted by Norbu Tsering. New composition inside the form is part of the record.

## How to listen

Ignore any studio harmony on a first hearing. Follow the drum. A change in the pattern is a change on stage. The vocal line is strophic and highly ornamented. It is closer to a recited story sung on a strong tune than to monastic dbyangs. The scale language, when the tune is a Central Tibetan one, is the pentatonic world of [[Scales and pentatonic modes]], worn smooth by oral variation.
