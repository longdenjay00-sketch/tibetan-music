# Cham

Cham ('cham) is masked ritual dance performed by trained monks or, in some Bon and village settings, by other ritual specialists. The masks are deities, protectors, and attendants. The dance is a visualization made public. It is not a play with a subplot, and it is not [[Ache Lhamo]].

## What you hear

The band is the monastic orchestra: long horns, gyaling shawms, conch, drums, and cymbals. There is no dranyen and no yangqin. The gyaling is the instrument that carries discrete-pitch rta melodies over the drones. Cymbals articulate the form. Ellingson argued that calling every other part "secondary" must not be read as "trivial." Each timbre has a job. The jobs are sorted in [[Ritual winds]] and [[Rhythm and rol mo]].

The dance and the music are rehearsed as one memory. A cue is a step and a stroke together. Watching without the sound, or extracting the sound as a concert piece, removes the thing the performers trained.

## When it happens

Houses perform cham on their own ritual calendar, often on a major festival day. Large public cham at a monastery courtyard is what most visitors see. The public is a witness. The rite is not organized as a ticketed drama, even when a modern festival schedules it for an audience.

Costumes and masks are consecrated objects in a traditional setting. Photography rules are local. Follow the monastery.

## How cham uses musical categories

The vocal music of the surrounding ritual may include all three types in [[Vocal styles]]. The dance music itself is largely instrumental. Do not expect a pentatonic folk tune, and do not expect the multiphonic chord of Gyuto unless that college's chant is part of the day's liturgy. A cham day and a tantric-college chant service are different events that can share a courtyard over a year.

## Sources
