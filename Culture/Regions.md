# Regions

"Tibetan music" is a family name. The practices below share Tibetic languages and a lot of religious history. Their tunes, tunings, and favorite dances are local. Learn the three provinces first. Then keep the Himalayan neighbors as neighbors.

## Ü-Tsang

Ü is the Lhasa region. Tsang is the region of Shigatse and Gyantse. Together they are the home of the documented urban genres: [[Nangma and toeshey]], the Shoton opera season, and the court [[Court gar and Lhasa classicism|gar]]. Collinge's pentatonic lexicon and his la-re-sol lute tuning describe specialist music of this center. See [[Scales and pentatonic modes]] and [[Dranyen]].

Central Tibetan folk song is often anhemitonic pentatonic. Some songs are hexatonic. A few are heptatonic. That is Collinge's count for the songs he studied. It is not a census of every village.

## Kham

Kham is the east: the cultural region across what are now eastern TAR, western Sichuan, and parts of Yunnan and Qinghai. Kham dance-song (khams gzhas) favors circle dances and long preludes. English listeners often remark on a wide, forceful vocal timbre. Treat the timbre as a style you learn by listening, not as a measurement this vault can quote. Warrior and heroic imagery is common in song texts. The Gesar epic is strongly associated with eastern bard traditions as well as with a wider Tibetan public. See [[Gesar and storytelling]].

## Amdo

Amdo is the northeast, largely in today's Qinghai and Gansu, with parts of Sichuan. The love-song tradition la gzhas and the lute-accompanied popular style rdung len (dunglen, "strum and sing") are the names a newcomer meets first. Dunglen is the bridge from picnic song to the modern Amdo video singer. See [[Folk song and dunglen]].

Amdo speech and Ü speech differ enough that a song lyric is not automatically portable. The musical difference is just as real.

## Himalayan Tibetic cultures

These are related, and they are not provinces of Lhasa.

| Place | What to keep separate |
| --- | --- |
| Ladakh | Own dranyen tuning, in fourths. Own song repertoire. Monasteries share liturgical families with Tibet. |
| Bhutan | The dramyin is a national instrument. Seven strings are common. Court and village repertoires are Bhutanese. Masked dance and monastic music belong to the Drukpa and related traditions. |
| Sikkim and Himalayan West Bengal | Shared instruments, local repertoire. |
| Nepal's Tibetan and Sherpa communities, and exile towns such as Boudhanath | Both old local practice and post-1959 exile practice. The Nepal Tibetan Lhamo Association is an exile opera company, studied for its drum patterns. |
| Spiti, Lahul, Dolpo, and similar high valleys | Liturgical sound travels with monasteries. Folk song is valley by valley. |

Collinge 1993 is the source for the contrast between Central Tibetan lute tuning and the fourths tuning of Ladakh and parts of western and southern Tibet. The Bhutanese seven-string instrument is the usual museum and reference description. See [[Dranyen]].

## A caution about maps

Political borders and musical regions do not match. Amdo singers may be citizens of Qinghai. A Ladakhi monastery may sound closer to a Tibetan liturgical family than a Lhasa pop song does. Name the region and the genre. "From Tibet" is not a musical description.
