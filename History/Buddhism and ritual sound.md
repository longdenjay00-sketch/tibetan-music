# Buddhism and ritual sound

From the later spread of Buddhism onward, the monastery is the institution that writes about music, trains specialists, and keeps a liturgy stable enough to compare across centuries. Secular music keeps moving beside it. This note is the religious stream. [[Court gar and Lhasa classicism]] is the courtly one.

## A liturgy is a repertoire

Each college has its own melodies for the same titles. An umdzé (dbu mdzad), the chant leader, carries that memory. In some Sakya houses a rol dpon plays the cymbals from the outline the leader beats on the drum. The categories those leaders use are [[How the music is organized|'don, rta, and dbyangs]]. The rhythmic system is [[Rhythm and rol mo]].

Instrumental music in this setting is called, in one scholastic phrase, sound produced by external conditions: rkyen gzhan las 'byung ba'i rol mo. It frames the chant. It does not replace it. The usual winds and percussion are in [[Ritual winds]] and [[Drums cymbals bell and damaru]].

## Sakya Pandita's treatise

Sakya Pandita Kunga Gyaltsen (1182–1251) wrote *Rol mo'i bstan bcos*, the Treatise on Music, in verse around 1200. It is the earliest Tibetan work that theorizes vocal music at any length. Three chapters, in Canzio's reading:

1. Melodic contour. Four kinds of nga ro, and how they combine.
2. The words, and what a text is for in a composition.
3. Performance: attitude, posture, and the faults that make a rendition fail.

Zhouyang Ma's 2025 translation of chapter 1 argues that the four nga ro adapt the four varṇa of the Indian *Nāṭyaśāstra*. That is an Indian technical idea put to work on Tibetan chant. It is not a raga system, and it is not the later pentatonic lexicon. A separate study of the treatise stresses the same gap from the other side: phonemes and melodic movement stay tied together, poetic meter carries the rhythm, and a scale or mode grid is simply not what the book is about.

The treatise was admired and, on Canzio's account, not the center of a living oral commentary in recent centuries. Working monks learn from an umdzé and from chant books. The books are described in [[Yang notation]]. A mes zhabs wrote a commentary that Ma translates with chapter 1. Canzio's 2019 book translates the treatise with commentary from the same scholarly neighborhood. Read the two editions as scholarship, not as two competing liturgies.

## Two Gelug voices

Gelug tantric colleges became the famous laboratory of very low chant. Study Buddhism, summarizing the colleges' own account, distinguishes two styles traced to visions of Tsongkhapa (1357–1419):

| Name | Tibetan | Sound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mountain-cracking voice | ri bo ral ba'i skad | Extremely low, flat, monotone |
| Ocean-rolling voice | chu gter 'khrog pa'i skad | Extremely low, undulating, with audible overtones |

Sera, Drepung, and Ganden use the ocean-rolling voice. Until the fifteenth Ganden throne-holder, Panchen Sonam Dragpa (1478–1554), both Gyume and Gyuto used the mountain-cracking voice. Gyume kept it. Gyuto took up the ocean-rolling voice. The physics of that second style are in [[Overtone chant]]. Most monastic chant, including most Gelug chant outside these colleges, is not multiphonic.

Gyume (rgyud smad, Lower Tantric College) and Gyuto (rgyud stod, Upper Tantric College) were founded in the fifteenth century. Their textbooks, protectors, and summer-retreat sites differ. The music differs with them.

## Bon

Bon monasteries chant, drum, and sound the gshang. Some instruments and some ritual ideas are shared with Buddhist houses. The melodies and the theology are not a subset of Gelug practice. If a recording is labeled only "Tibetan monks," the order is still unknown.
