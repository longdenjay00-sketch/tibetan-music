# Court, gar, and Lhasa classicism

Lhasa, from the seventeenth century, is where a secular classical practice becomes visible in writing: ceremonial gar, the song genre nangma, the dance songs called toeshey, and a state season of opera. The religious colleges do not stop. This is the other professionalism.

## Gar

Gar is ceremonial dance-music for ritual and state occasions. Tibetan accounts usually place its roots west of Ü, in the same broad direction as toeshey. It nearly disappeared as a court office in the twentieth century. In exile it was rebuilt under Pa-sang Dondrub (Pa-sangs Don-grub), born 1918, the last gar-dpon to have served a ruling Dalai Lama in Tibet. Long instrumental preludes are part of this style. Purely instrumental music is otherwise rare. Songs lead. See [[Nangma and toeshey]].

## Nangma

Nangma (nang ma) is the slow, ornamental chamber song of Lhasa. The name is widely connected to Persian and Kashmiri *naghma*, "melody." Muslim musicians from Baltistan, Kashmir, and Central Asia did work in Lhasa. That West Asian and Himalayan contact is part of the origin story. It does not make nangma a raga tradition.

The New Grove article on Tibetan music gives a more precise instrument story. The Lhasa minister Doring Pandita (rDo ring bstan 'dzin dpal 'byor) is said to have brought a yangqin from China in 1793 and to have set it beside the lute, the flute, and the fiddle. He is also credited with bringing Chinese gongche notation into this repertoire. Tibetans called that notation kung khre'i phu'u. It stayed in use into the 1950s. [[Yang notation]] compares it with monastic curves. [[Piwang lingbu and yangqin]] describes the dulcimer.

TIPA's teaching history dates the entertainment itself earlier, to the time of the Fifth Dalai Lama in the seventeenth century, and treats the poetry of the Sixth, Tsangyang Gyatso, as a high point of the song texts. The two accounts fit together if you separate the songs from the later ensemble. Songs and picnics can be older than the 1793 dulcimer band. Grove is the source for the dulcimer date. TIPA is the source for how the exile institute tells the poetic history.

## Toeshey

Toeshey (stod gzhas) means songs of Tö, western Tibet. In Lhasa the name also covers dance songs of central Tibetan origin that entered the same parties. They are faster than nangma and built for dancing. The same players often do both. Detail is in [[Nangma and toeshey]].

## Opera as a state season

[[Ache Lhamo]] is older than the Ganden Phodrang government as a popular theatre, if the Thangtong Gyalpo tradition is taken at its word. Under that government it also became a season. Troupes were called to the Norbulingka, the Dalai Lama's summer palace, for the Shoton (zho ston), the yogurt festival, after the summer retreat. A 1958 list, discussed in the modern literature on lhamo, counts major and minor troupes on that roster. The accompaniment stayed drum and cymbals.

## What "classical" means here

Calling nangma the classical music of Tibet is a scholar's convenience. It means a named urban repertoire, specialist players, a somewhat fixed ensemble, and a notation habit. It does not mean a conservatory theory of harmony. The theory that does exist for this repertoire is the pentatonic lexicon in [[Scales and pentatonic modes]], written down by Tibetan musicologists very late, in the late twentieth century. Collinge's point in 1996/97 is that the lexicon was still being built.
