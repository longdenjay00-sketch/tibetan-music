# Plateau and empire

Written Tibetan music theory begins late. The earliest substantial treatise is Sakya Pandita's *Rol mo'i bstan bcos*, from about 1200. Anything earlier is archaeology, later memory, and the general history of the empire. Treat those centuries as context, not as a repertoire you can reconstruct.

## Before the empire

People have lived on the plateau for millennia. Song, dance, and drumming do not wait for Buddhism. Bon, the other organized Tibetan religion, has its own ritual sound, including the flat bell called gshang. Bon is a living tradition. It is not a preface that Buddhism erased. See [[Sacred and secular]].

Western writers sometimes suggest that cave resonance shaped later overtone chant. That is a suggestion in the secondary literature, repeated by popular surveys. It is not an acoustic finding. The measured account of multiphonic chant is in [[Overtone chant]].

## The imperial period

The Tibetan empire runs from the seventh century into the ninth. Standard political history places Songtsen Gampo in the seventh century, Trisong Detsen and the founding of Samye in the eighth, and a collapse of imperial Buddhism in the mid-ninth. Music rides inside that story only at the edges:

- Court life had ceremony, dance, and song. Later genres such as [[Court gar and Lhasa classicism|gar]] claim western and courtly roots. The claim is a tradition about origin, not a seventh-century score.
- Indian and Chinese Buddhist teachers entered Tibet in this period. Instruments that later belong to the monastery, especially the hand-bell, the hourglass damaru, and the thighbone trumpet, have Indian tantric lineages. Ellingson documented those instrument lineages. The melodies they play in Tibet are Tibetan.
- There is no imperial treatise that lists scales. Do not project the pentatonic modes in [[Scales and pentatonic modes]] backward onto the empire.

## What survives the gap

After the empire broke, monastic Buddhism was rebuilt from the late tenth century, the period Tibetans call the later spread. From that rebuilding come the orders whose chant you can still compare: Nyingma, Sakya, Kagyu, and, from the fifteenth century, Gelug. The next note, [[Buddhism and ritual sound]], starts there.

Secular song across the same centuries is even thinner in writing. The lute, the dance-song, and the epic bard are old. Their documented urban forms are much later. [[Folk song and dunglen]] and [[Gesar and storytelling]] keep those streams separate from the imperial court.
