# The twentieth century and the diaspora

The break in 1950–1959 splits institutions. It does not split the music into a pure exile stream and a replaced homeland stream. Both sides teach, record, and change the repertoire. A vault note can name the institutions. It cannot rank their authenticity.

## Exile institutions

The Fourteenth Dalai Lama founded the body that became the Tibetan Institute of Performing Arts in August 1959. TIPA's own history places the founding at Kalimpong, under the name Tibetan Music, Dance, and Drama Society, and the move to McLeod Ganj in 1961. One encyclopedia entry places the founding at McLeod Ganj. Prefer TIPA for the sequence: Kalimpong in August 1959, McLeod Ganj in 1961. A later write-up specifies 18 August 1959. The day is less settled than the year.

TIPA was built to hold opera, dance, and music from Ü-Tsang, Kham, and Amdo, and to train performers who would otherwise have had no court and no Shoton. It still teaches three lhamo lineages and the plays tied to them. See [[Ache Lhamo]]. It also teaches nangma and toeshey. Gar was rebuilt there under the last court gar-dpon. See [[Court gar and Lhasa classicism]].

Monasteries re-founded in India, Nepal, and Bhutan carried chant college by college. Gyuto and Gyume in exile are the source of the recordings that introduced multiphonic chant to laboratories and to listeners abroad. Huston Smith recorded Gyuto monks in Dalhousie in July 1967. That tape is the one MIT analyzed. [[Listening map]] lists it. [[Overtone chant]] explains it.

## On the plateau

After 1959, state ensembles and, later, a popular industry grew inside Tibet and in Amdo and Kham districts of Qinghai and Sichuan. Nangma moved into urban teahouses and then karaoke. That fact is in every recent survey of Lhasa. The songs did not become Western pop by changing rooms.

Amdo produced a cassette and video scene strong enough to travel on local buses and then beyond China. Names that English-language surveys repeat include Sherten (Sherab Tendzin) and Yadong. Inside the TAR, singers such as Dadon, Jampa Tsering, and the band Vajara (Tian Chu) mark the rock and pop decades. Han Chinese state performance made Tseten Dolma famous in the 1960s. Yadong and Sherten reached audiences outside China. This list is a door, not a canon. Styles change faster than a note can.

Language of the songs stays mostly Tibetan. Arrangements pick up keyboard, drum machine, guitar, and film-song harmony. When a recording has those, you are hearing the popular industry. When it has dranyen, piwang, lingbu, and yangqin, you are hearing the older ensemble, sometimes playing the same tune.

## What left, and what was invented abroad

Indian ghazal and film song are ordinary listening in the diaspora. So is Anglophone rock. Rangzen Shonu is the early exile rock name English sources cite. None of that cancels TIPA or the monasteries.

A separate American invention, the rubbed "singing bowl," was marketed as Tibetan from the 1970s. It has its own history, and it is not this repertoire. [[What is not Tibetan music]] keeps the line.

## How to listen across the break

Ask three questions of any twentieth-century recording:

1. Which region and which institution: a named monastery, TIPA, a Lhasa nangma hall, an Amdo video singer?
2. Which accompaniment: ritual winds, drum and cymbals, the secular quartet, or a studio band?
3. Which language of pitch: contour chant, pentatonic song, or harmony added in the studio?

The answers place the recording. They do not require you to discard it.
